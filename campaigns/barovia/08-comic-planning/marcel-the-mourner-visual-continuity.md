# Character Visual Continuity - Marcel: The Mourner

A worked example of designing a disguise for a character who cannot speak, and of keeping that
disguise recognisable as the same person underneath.

Subject: Marcel, a changeling bard of the College of Creation, in the face he wears for a mission
investigating young adults disappearing from the town of Vallaki.

## Why This Character Is A Useful Visual-Continuity Case

Marcel does not speak. He communicates through mime, staged tableaux, music, and prepared Magic
Mouth stones. That makes his appearance load-bearing in a way most characters' is not: what he
chooses to look like *is* his opening line, so every costume decision has to carry meaning that
dialogue would normally carry.

He is also a changeling, which means the appearance is a racial Shape Change rather than a costume.
That draws a hard and useful line through the design:

| Shape Change alters | Shape Change does not alter |
| --- | --- |
| Face, age, colouring, hair | Clothing and equipment |
| Height and build, within one size class | Statistics and abilities |
| Voice | Anything carried |

Everything on the "does not alter" side is a physical object that can be taken, broken, dropped, or
left behind. That distinction turns out to drive most of the good design choices below.

## The Thesis

**The goth is armor, and the face underneath is still sixteen and wrong for the room.**

Goth styling is about looking dangerous, adult, and apart. Worn by a face that cannot sell any of
those, it becomes something else — which is what real goth teenagers actually look like, and it is
poignant rather than cool. He is not intimidating. He is a child in a costume that is trying very
hard, and the trying is the point.

The mission concerns young adults vanishing off the streets. He has made himself into one, which is
both a disguise and an offer.

## Design Elements

### Rendered by Shape Change

- **The face.** Soft, round, unfinished at the jaw. Reads a year or two younger than the age he is
  claiming. Chalk-white, moon-pale. Prominent ears he has not grown into.
- **The hair.** Dark, fine, straight, shaggy. Long over the forehead and ears, falling into his
  eyes, pushed back constantly and never staying there.
- **The eye.** A violet star marking at the outer corner of the right eye, *grown* rather than
  painted, drawn downward into a long tear-track — as though he had wept it crooked and never fixed
  it. He did not weep. He grew it that way.
- **Height and build.** Adjusted down within the same size class. Slight. Takes up no doorway.

### Physical objects, which he must actually carry

- **The glasses.** Heavy frames, slightly too large for the face. He does not need them — a
  changeling can simply fix his eyes — so they are pure affectation, which is both very teenage and
  very in character. **Their real value is that they can be lost.** A pair of a missing boy's
  glasses on the ground is one of the most legible images available for "something happened here."
- **The coat.** His old star-embroidered robe, turned and re-dyed black, and re-stitched. The
  constellations were not removed; they **collapsed into polyhedra**. Same silver thread, same
  catch-the-light trick, but the star-fields have folded into hard geometric solids. In a lit room
  he is a boy in a plain black coat; in a dark street he is faintly, uncomfortably full of angular
  shapes that used to be a sky.
- **The gloves.** White, with black fingerless lace mitts pulled over them. When he wants a gesture
  to read clearly he strips the black off one hand and the white palm underneath does the talking.
  Chipped black lacquer on the nails beneath, against the pristine white.
- **Kohl**, applied badly on purpose. Smudged, uneven, one eye heavier than the other. A changeling
  could make it perfect, so the imperfection is a decision: a kid who did it himself, in bad light,
  without help.
- **Jewelry.** Too many silver rings, a black ribbon choker, and a small tarnished locket that is
  conspicuously empty. Anyone who opens the locket finds nothing in it. That is the point of it.
- **The concertina.** A small hexagonal squeezebox of dark aged wood and black folded bellows with
  tarnished silver fittings, carried in both hands rather than stowed.
- **The lute across his back**, on a plain leather strap with a black mourning ribbon knotted at the
  pegbox, ends left trailing. Carried, deliberately not played.
- **A twenty-sided die on a cord**, thumbed constantly, worn where a holy symbol would go. Two faces
  he can show people without speaking: the worst one and the best one.
- **A hand-drawn map on squared paper**, folded soft — the streets where people vanished, gridded
  and annotated in a careful, unhurried hand.
- **Boots**: scuffed, laced wrong, road mud not cleaned off.

## Why The Concertina

It earns its place on three counts, and the reasoning generalises to any instrument choice.

**It breathes.** A concertina has lungs and works them audibly — the wheeze between notes is part
of the instrument. Handing it to the one character who will not use his own voice is a pointed
choice. Something in his hands is doing the breathing he declines to do.

**It is the right class of instrument.** A lute is a performance instrument; you carry it to be
watched. A squeezebox is a street instrument — small, cheap-looking, wheezy, played sitting on steps
for coins. The previous face brought a lute to a hilltop and expected an audience. This one brings
what a busking teenager would own.

**The shape rhymes.** Hexagonal bellows, folded flat planes, angular geometry — the same visual
family as the polyhedra on the coat and the die at his throat. The whole design is built out of
folded solids.

The lute does not disappear; it *moves*. Slung across his back with a mourning ribbon at the pegbox,
it reads as an instrument he has deliberately stopped playing, which is its own statement.

## Why The Polyhedra Are Not A Joke

A tabletop character wearing dice-shaped ornament risks reading as a wink at the audience. Three
things stop it.

**The setting already supports it.** This campaign world runs on Tarokka card divination, so
fortune-telling objects are native furniture. A boy who reads his future in rolled polyhedra needs
no more justification than the card-readers do.

**The geometry is real occult material.** The Platonic solids are genuine sacred geometry; Plato
assigned the icosahedron to water. Read as alchemical or astrological instruments, they belong.

**The shape rhymes with what came before.** An icosahedron in silhouette is a star-burst. The
character's old star-embroidered robe and his new polyhedral one are the same visual family, which
is exactly why the constellations can collapse into solids without the garment ever stopping being
his.

## Recognition Threads

The most important continuity rule here. Four elements are deliberately preserved from his previous
appearance so that anyone who knows him can half-place him and not quite say why:

1. The violet star at the right eye.
2. The white gloves under everything.
3. The lute.
4. The moon-pallor.

Keeping these turns the disguise into a *performance* rather than a swap — a much more interesting
thing to draw, because the audience can see both people at once.

Neither is strictly better. The painted version keeps the setting mood; the pencil version serves
the face. Generation brief and full findings in `marcel-the-mourner-image-prompt.md`.

**Known deviation in both:** the white gloves are absent. Repeated generation passes could not add
them without distorting the hands, because the model conflates white gloves with the character's
already chalk-white skin. They remain canon in text — and the moment that actually needs them,
stripping the lace off one hand so the white palm can make a gesture read, does not depend on the
picture.
